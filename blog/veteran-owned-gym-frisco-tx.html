Veteran-Owned Isn’t a Label. It’s the Whole Story.

July 16, 2026 8 min read
← Back to Blog

People ask what "veteran-owned" actually means at Black Iron Athletics. Most gyms that use those words put a flag on the wall and call it done. For me, the honest answer runs through Afghanistan, a broken back, a workout named after a Marine who died right behind me, and a gym on Main Street in Frisco.

So here's the whole story. — Mike Manning, owner

The Smallest Guy in the Room

I've been an athlete my entire life — and I've been the small athlete my entire life. In high school I was a wrestler and a powerlifter, which is another way of saying I got comfortable early with being the lightest guy on the platform and making up the difference with work.

Shortly after high school, I joined the Marine Corps. I became a signals intelligence support team leader. If you don't know what that means: intelligence drives operations. Without it, you're fighting in the dark against an enemy you can't see. My job was to make sure my Marines never had to.

Afghanistan, 2009

I deployed to Afghanistan in 2009. Within the first few weeks in country, I got hurt while training — a severe injury to my left leg that made it hard to walk, hard to do most things. I stayed in country and continued the mission.

During operations later in the deployment, I broke my back. That one was worse than the leg. I was stubborn and, honestly, a little hard-headed at the time — so I stayed again. I kept serving as a team leader in support of 2nd Light Armored Reconnaissance and 2nd Force Recon for six months, and when 4th LAR and 4th Force Recon relieved them, I stayed on and worked with them for another four. Essentially two tours combined into one.

By the end I was beat up and broken down, weighing close to 115 pounds and carrying close to 100 pounds of gear at any given point. But the weight I actually remember carrying was my team — my Marines, and the Marines we supported. My mindset was simple: survive one more day. My job was simpler: get my guys home safely.

I got back, and almost immediately left for another deployment, this time to the Philippines. I was still in bad shape — physically, mentally, emotionally. I continued the mission anyway, supported my team, and pushed where I could to be the best Marine I could be. It's what I knew how to do.

The Body That Came Home

When I got back and life settled into the normal rhythm of the fleet, I finally took stock of what those years had cost. My foot no longer worked properly because of the leg injury. My back made it difficult to walk, to run, to do much of anything. I wasn't squatting. I wasn't doing the functional movements that had defined my whole life as an athlete.

I gave everything I had to healing — to recovering, recuperating, getting my body back to where the Marine Corps needed it to be. It wasn't enough. In 2012, I was medically retired, and I moved back to Texas.

If you've ever lost the thing that told you who you were, you know exactly what that season felt like.

Learning to Be an Athlete Again

While I was in, this thing called CrossFit had gotten popular. When I got out, I found something in it I didn't expect: community and camaraderie — everything I was missing from the Marine Corps. It gave me a place to start over as an athlete.

And I do mean start over. I had to learn how to run, jump, and squat again — all of it, this time with the modifications and complications that come with being a medically retired combat veteran. My body was not the body that went in. But I still wanted to be an athlete. The gym was where I was happy again.

So I turned down a very high-paying job and went into coaching full time. People thought I was crazy. They weren't entirely wrong. But some decisions you make with a spreadsheet, and some you make because you finally feel like yourself again.

MarineWOD and Sergeant Ryan Lane

Around that time I founded a project called MarineWOD. The idea was to use storytelling to honor Marines who didn't come home — to take who they were and what they lived through, and personify it in a workout of the day. It grew nationwide. People I'd never met would reach out and ask me to write the story of a Marine they'd lost, and I'd turn their memory into something you could feel in your hands and lungs.

The first one I ever wrote was for Sergeant Ryan Lane. He died right behind me in Afghanistan. That impact stayed with me for years, and it's still felt today. Writing his workout was the best way I knew to honor and remember him.

The very first MarineWOD event was the Sgt Lane WOD, and we flew his mom out from Pittsburgh to be there and witness it. Every movement in that workout meant something. Burpees were hitting the deck and getting back up again. Kettlebell swings were filling a sandbag in a hole and swinging it up and out. Deadlifts were picking your buddy up and getting him to safety — even when he was heavy.

MarineWOD doesn't exist anymore. What it taught me does: a workout can carry a story, and a gym can carry a purpose.

Finding Black Iron

MarineWOD led me to a young gym called Black Iron CrossFit, six months to a year into its life. I came on to coach, became head coach, and eventually an owner. I'd always wanted my own gym, and honestly, I thought this was a stepping stone to it.

Then I fell in love with the community. I found my place. I found my home. I found my sense of purpose again outside the Marine Corps — helping people become athletes and get through whatever challenges they're carrying, physical, mental, or emotional. This became my new mission and my ministry at the same time.

I eventually had the opportunity to buy out my business partners, and I've been the sole owner of what is now Black Iron Athletics since 2018. We've been going strong ever since — and we're getting ready for our next chapter: a bigger facility with a bigger focus on nutrition and recovery, built on the same core principles and methodology we've run for years. Until the day those new doors open, you'll find us where we've always been — 279 Main St, Suite 122, in Frisco.

What Veteran-Owned Actually Means Here

It means this gym was built by somebody who had to rebuild. Every member who walks in carrying something — an old injury, a lost identity, a body that doesn't work the way it used to — is walking into a gym owned by someone who has stood exactly there. Nobody gets written off here. Our mission is the same one I found on the other side of my own story: we help everyday people become everyday athletes. No matter where you're starting from, what you've been through, or what you're carrying, we help guide you to a happier, healthier life through movement, nutrition, community, good habits, and purpose.

It also means two concrete things:

  • Military, veterans, and first responders get a discount on membership. Ask your coach about it at your free consultation.
  • Our adaptive athletics program is completely free. We meet every other Wednesday at 11am, and anyone is welcome — regardless of disability, ability, or ability to pay. The program is sponsored by Texas Parasport, and it exists so we stay grounded in the work that started it all.

One More Day

In Afghanistan, my mindset was survive one more day. These days I ask something easier of myself, and of every member here: show up one more day. String enough of those together and you get a life back. I'm proof.

If you're a veteran in Frisco looking for a gym where somebody understands what you're carrying, come find me — I'm on the floor most days. And if you've never served a day in your life, the invitation is exactly the same, because this place was never just for us. Book a free consultation, or read about what your first week looks like. I'll see you on the floor.

Written by Mike Manning

U.S. Marine Corps veteran, owner and head coach of Black Iron Athletics in Frisco, TX. Over 20 years of coaching experience with certifications in USA Weightlifting, personal training, and more.
