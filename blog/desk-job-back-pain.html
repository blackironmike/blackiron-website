Sitting Is a Slow Death: A Coach’s Case for Standing Up

September 8, 2026 5 min read By Mike Manning

I can usually tell what a new member does for a living before they tell me. Not from the truck they park out front or the watch on their wrist. From the way they move in their first warm-up.

Somebody settles into a squat and their heels want to leave the floor. Their hips fight them the whole way down. Hamstrings like guitar strings. Glutes that have forgotten they have a job. A low back that volunteers for everybody else’s work and then complains about it for days. When I see that combination, I don’t ask what sport they played in high school. I ask where they sit.

The Pattern I Keep Seeing

In members with desk jobs, the same four problems show up together so often I treat them as one problem: tight hips, tight hamstrings, a weak low back, and underdeveloped glutes. And every one of them is at the mercy of the same habit. Sitting.

Most people sit through a commute, then sit through a desk job. If you work from home, you didn’t escape it. You just traded the commute for an earlier start in the same chair. Calls stack up, lunch happens at the keyboard, and by the time you shut the laptop your body has spent the whole day folded up. The walk from the coffee maker to the desk is not movement. It’s a rumor of movement.

I have a blunt way of putting it: sitting is a slow death. I don’t say that to scare anybody. I say it because of what I keep seeing in members with desk jobs. In my experience, the people who spend the most time in a chair are the ones fighting that pattern the hardest when they train. That matters double if you’re the guy with a demanding job and kids who climb on you like playground equipment. You can’t afford to get hurt. Which means you can’t afford to ignore this.

My Back Made Me Deal With It

I’d love to tell you I got ahead of this out of pure coaching wisdom. The truth is my back forced my hand. It was in a bad place, bad enough that I stopped treating the desk hours of my life as neutral and started treating them as part of the problem. So I bought a standing desk with my own money and rebuilt how I work around it. These days I stand for nearly the entire workday.

I wrote the whole story up on Every Dad Carry, my gear review site, including the full review of the desk I paid for myself. Quick and honest disclosure: Every Dad Carry is my site, and some of the links there are affiliate links, which means I may earn a commission if you buy through them.

I’m not going to retell the review here, and I’m not going to pretend a desk is a training program. This is a coaching post, not a gear post. The gear details live over there. What belongs here is the coaching case.

What I’d Tell You to Do About It

Start with the free stuff. Notice the shape your day puts you in, and interrupt it. Take calls on your feet. Stand for the parts of your work that don’t need a keyboard. Get up between meetings, even if it’s just to refill your water. None of that costs a dime, and all of it beats another unbroken hour in the chair. Set yourself up so the better option is the automatic one instead of the one you have to remember on willpower alone.

Then go after the four things I named at the top. Work your hips. Train the hinge. Give your glutes and hamstrings real jobs again, so your low back can stop covering shifts it was never scheduled for. Build that low back up patiently instead of testing it cold. You don’t need my exact program. You need consistency, and a plan that respects where you’re starting from instead of where you wish you were.

And a line I want to be clear about: if your back genuinely hurts, real pain, the sharp kind, the constant kind, the getting-worse kind, see a doctor before you see me. I’m a strength coach, not a physician, and nothing in this post is medical advice. But if you’re simply stiff, achy, and folded up from the chair, I’ve laid out the full case on standing, sitting, and my own back so you can read it and make your own call.

For My Fellow Veterans

One more thing, because this part matters to me. I’m a disabled Marine Corps veteran, and Black Iron is a veteran-owned gym. If you’re a veteran, there’s a process through VA vocational rehab programs that can cover a standing desk for those who qualify. I wrote a plain-English walkthrough of how that process works, the general steps only, so you can see whether it applies to you without digging through it alone. It costs nothing to read and nothing to ask, and the worst answer you can get is no.

And while we’re on the subject: we run a free program for disabled veterans here at the gym in Frisco. No affiliate link on that one, no pitch, and no catch. Just a place to train with people who get it. If that’s you, come see us.

The Hours Outside the Gym

Everything above comes back to a belief that shapes how we coach: the one hour you spend in the gym matters less than the twenty-three you spend outside it. Nutrition. Sleep. Daily habits. How you sit, and how often you get up.

Our mission is simple. We help everyday people become everyday athletes. An everyday athlete isn’t built in sixty minutes a day. The build happens in the quiet choices nobody claps for: what goes on your plate, when the phone goes down at night, and what shape your body holds between breakfast and dinner.

So here’s your homework from coach, and it’s the easiest set I’ll ever give you. Stand up. Move more than you did yesterday. Come train with us when you’re ready. Your family needs you durable for a long, long time. Don’t let a chair be the thing that takes that from you.

Written by Mike Manning

U.S. Marine Corps veteran, owner and head coach of Black Iron Athletics in Frisco, TX. Over 20 years of coaching experience with certifications in USA Weightlifting, personal training, and more.
