How We Program Strength Training in Frisco, TX

August 1, 2026 8 min read By Mike Manning

Ask a gym what you are doing on Thursday. If the honest answer is that nobody knows yet, because a coach will write it Wednesday night, you have learned everything you need to know about your next six months. Some days hard, some days easy, all of it pointed at nothing in particular.

Here is our answer to that question. Everything that goes on the board at Black Iron Athletics has a purpose and a design behind it. Nothing is random. We write ten weeks at a time, and I can tell you today what the shape of week nine looks like.

This is the long version of how we program strength training here in Frisco. The short version, with the same cycle map, lives on our programs page.

The trap of never repeating a workout

There is an old-school idea in this industry that variety is the product. Something different every day. Never the same workout twice. It sounds exciting, it fills a room, and it is a mistake.

Your body adapts to what it sees repeatedly. If the stimulus changes every single day, there is nothing to adapt to. You get sore, you get tired, you get a workout — and six months later your squat is the same number it was in January. Worse, you have nothing to compare it against. No lift you have done often enough to know whether it actually moved.

The other ditch is just as real. Run the same five lifts on the same days forever and people get bored and quit. We spent more than a decade as a CrossFit affiliate before we built our own system, and the reason we moved on was not that the training was bad. It was that we wanted control over the design.

The balance between repetition and variety is the whole craft. That is the job.

Linear progression vs periodization, and why we use both

Linear progression is the simple one. Add a little weight to the bar each week, hold the reps steady, get stronger in a straight line. It works beautifully — right up until it doesn’t. Every lifter eventually runs out of straight line.

Periodization is the grown-up version. You plan in waves. Volume and intensity rise and fall on purpose across a block of weeks, with lighter weeks built in before you need them instead of after you break. It is how people keep progressing for years instead of months.

We are not picking a side. A cycle here progresses linearly inside each wave, is periodized across the ten weeks, and carries enough variety in the conditioning and accessory work that you never dread the drive over.

Repetition with intent is how I explain it to new members. We repeat the lifts and we repeat the tests, so you get a real chance to adapt to something. Things are not constantly changing on you. You learn the movement, you build appropriately, you adapt appropriately, and you see progress week after week.

Progress you can see is what keeps you showing up

That last part matters more than any percentage on a spreadsheet. When you can see the number move, motivation stays high. When motivation stays high, you keep showing up. Showing up is what actually produces results.

Falling off is the real enemy. Not a suboptimal set-and-rep scheme. A good program you run for two years beats a perfect program you abandon in six weeks, every time. So we design against the two things that actually break people: boredom on one side, burnout on the other. If the barbell is new to you, start with our beginner guide to strength training.

What a ten-week cycle actually looks like

Here is the shape of a recent cycle. We called it Last Rep. The week numbers are the point, not the calendar — every cycle we write follows this arc.

Week one is intro week. You learn the lifts, and coaches watch how you move before anything gets heavy. Week two is test week. You find your numbers on every main lift and run the benchmark workouts we come back to at the end.

From there, everything runs off your training max, which is ninety percent of what you tested. Not your true max. Ninety percent of it, and it stays the same for all ten weeks.

Why lower it on purpose? Because a max you hit once on a good day is not a weight you can train on. The training max is a number you can hit for reps on a Tuesday, after a bad night of sleep, with your form still intact. It is the difference between training and testing, and confusing the two is how people get hurt.

Weeks three through five build in waves: fives, then threes, then the 5/3/1 wave. A strength block reads something like 65 percent for five, 75 percent for five, then 85 percent for a push set — max quality reps, one or two left in the tank. Never grinding. If your bar speed drops, the weight is too heavy. That is a rule, not a suggestion, and it is what your coach is watching from across the floor.

Week six deloads. Weeks seven through nine run the same wave again with every percentage five points heavier than the first pass. Week ten retests every lift and all three benchmarks against week two — same movements, same standards, same order — so the comparison is honest.

The deload is planned around your life, not just your training

This is the part of the cycle I am proudest of, and it is the part most gyms never think about.

Why the deload lands where it does

Week six of that cycle was back-to-school week. That was not an accident. We plan the lighter week for the week your life gets heavier — so nothing gets missed, nothing gets skipped, and you come back in week seven ready to push instead of digging out of a hole.

We look at the calendar before we write the cycle. Back-to-school week in Frisco is chaos in a lot of houses. New schedules, new practices, forms to sign, a kid who needs picking up at a time that did not exist last week. Something has to give, and for most people the thing that gives is the gym.

So we made the gym the easy thing that week. You still come in. You still keep the habit. The loads are just lighter and the sessions are shorter on purpose. Then week seven arrives and you are ready to push, instead of trying to remember where you left off.

That is what I mean when I say we program for our athletes’ lives outside the gym, not just the hour inside it. When you miss a week, you do not really lose the week. You lose the momentum, and momentum is the expensive part.

Monday’s strength work is built on the Olympic lifts. Tuesday is gymnastics and pulling. Wednesday presses. Friday squats. Saturday is the long partner workout, which is the day the room feels most like a room full of your people.

Thursday is deliberately the light day, and it is the one people question most. Handstand work, accessory volume for the muscle you want, and then Zone 2 aerobic work at a conversational pace. Conversational means you can talk the entire time. That is the point of the session, not a suggestion to go easy on yourself. Easy aerobic work is what lets the heavy days stay heavy.

Inside a single session

Every class runs the same anatomy: warm-up, then skill or gymnastics, then strength, then conditioning. Each block is on a clock.

The time caps are not about tidiness. They protect the intent of each piece. Strength does not get squeezed into the last eight minutes because the warm-up ran long. Conditioning stays conditioning instead of turning into a second strength session at half speed. And class starts and finishes when it says it will, which matters when you have a call at eight.

Three levels, one floor

Every session is written with three levels. Level 1 is Learn it. Level 2 is Build it. Level 3 is Test it. Same session, same clock, same coaching, three honest doors into it.

That is how a first-timer and someone with ten years of barbell work train side by side without either one compromising. The system flexes so you do not have to.

Your levels get locked in during intro week, once you have felt the movements out with a coach watching. They are not a permanent label, either. You can be a Level 3 on the squat and a Level 1 on gymnastics in the same week, and most people are. See how it plays out in our group classes, and if you are training past 40, read why the approach changes.

Our benchmarks are ours

The three tests we run in week two and again in week ten are Black Iron originals. We wrote them. You will not find them in anyone else’s program, and we do not borrow someone else’s hero workout and call it a test.

Humble Beginnings Descending ladder

Dumbbell thrusters, box jump overs, pull-ups. The reps come down as the fatigue goes up. It is named for what it does to your ego in week two.

Forged in Fire Ascending ladder

Rower calories and burpees, climbing every round. A pacing test. Go out hot and the last round teaches you something.

Breaking Ground Heavy plus skill

Deadlifts, double unders, handstand push-ups, five rounds. Strength under fatigue, with a skill that falls apart first when you are tired.

They are named, written down, and repeated on purpose. We do not invent a fresh test at the end of the cycle and call it progress. Same workout, same standards, same movements, ten weeks apart.

That is how progress stops being a feeling and becomes a number. You either did more work in the same time or you did not, and either answer tells your coach something useful about the next ten weeks.

“What if I join in week six?”

Then you join in week six. This is the question we get most, and the answer is boring on purpose.

Everyone starts the same way: a free consult, then Foundations, a week of one-on-one coaching where you learn the basics, set a baseline, and get your levels sorted. After that you step into the cycle wherever it happens to be. If that is a deload week, you learn the movements at a sane load, which is a fine way to begin. If it is week nine, you follow the same percentages off your own numbers.

The three levels handle the rest, and a new cycle comes around every ten weeks, so a full one is never far off. Waiting for a clean start date is how six months disappear.
